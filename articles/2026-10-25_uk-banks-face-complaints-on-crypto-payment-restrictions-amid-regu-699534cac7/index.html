UK Banks Face Complaints on Crypto Payment Restrictions Amid Regulatory Uncertainty

Presence Editorial · October 25, 2026 · 4 min read

Stand With Crypto documented 10,092 complaints lodged by 9,803 individuals regarding restrictions on cryptocurrency payments over a 90-day period. These submissions highlight customer confusion as some lenders block transfers to digital asset platforms despite government initiatives aimed at attracting businesses in the sector.

Lloyds, Barclays and NatWest collectively accounted for 5,144 of these complaints, representing approximately 51% of all submissions received. Customers reported encountering payment restrictions even at institutions that had not publicly declared specific crypto payment limits, though it is noted that these complaints represent self-selected data rather than verified rejection rates across the entire banking sector. While the UK plans to introduce broader regulation for more crypto activities in 2027, current bank payment restrictions remain a separate issue from those future regulatory frameworks.

Major Banks Lead Volume of Crypto Payment Disputes

According to a report by advocacy group Stand With Crypto UK, 9,803 individuals utilized its online complaint tool between May and August 2026 to contest blocked or restricted transfers. These submissions originated from all 650 parliamentary constituencies, with Lloyds Banking Group, Barclays, and NatWest receiving the highest volumes of complaints.

Lloyds Banking Group accounted for 2,297 complaints covering customers of its Lloyds, Halifax, and Bank of Scotland brands. Barclays followed with 1,569 complaints, while NatWest Group received 1,278. Together, these three banking groups attracted 5,144 complaints, equivalent to approximately 51% of the total submissions.

The report indicates that HSBC UK handled over 1,000 submissions. In contrast, Virgin Money logged 103 cases and the Co-operative Bank tallied 76. Lloyds raises specific issues for the campaign since its examination of banking policies, completed by August 28, identified no publicly declared limits on cryptocurrency transfers.

Although Revolut was listed among institutions lacking published restrictions in the review, clients faced payment hurdles. The bank's support details note that specific cryptocurrency exchanges are not supported. This nuance is vital for users seeking to determine which financial entities authorize payments to regulated crypto firms, as a bank might approve some transfers but block others after fraud checks, internal risk evaluations, or limits on specific recipients.

Stand With Crypto gathered submissions from individuals utilizing its tool without normalizing data for each bank's customer base or total payment volume, meaning the data does not prove Lloyds rejects a larger share of cryptocurrency payments compared to rivals. The initiative highlighted clear distinctions between financial institutions that disclose transaction caps and those banning crypto-related transfers entirely.

Santander UK enforced a ceiling of £1,000 per transfer alongside a monthly cap of £3,000, whereas NatWest set daily limits at £1,000 and 30-day totals at £5,000. Barclays and HSBC UK allowed transfers reaching £2,500 individually, with an aggregate restriction of £10,000 across a 30-day window.

Starling Bank, Chase UK, TSB, Metro Bank, Virgin Money and the Co-operative Bank were flagged as entities banning cryptocurrency transactions. Data from the campaign indicates that institutions displaying explicit transaction limits received 6,198 of the total complaints, representing roughly 61% of all submissions. Another 2,842 cases involved providers with no stated public restrictions and 989 instances linked to banks enforcing outright bans.

Barclays maintains that its limitations serve to shield clients from crypto-related fraud and monetary loss, noting that financial institutions must evaluate risks associated with payments for criminal activity. Stand With Crypto contends that broad prohibitions may hinder valid transactions at companies registered with the Financial Conduct Authority. These grievances emerge as the United Kingdom readies a fresh regulatory structure for digital assets.

During an announcement in December 2025, HM Treasury expressed the government's goal of positioning Britain as a premier hub for digital asset enterprises while enhancing safeguards for consumers. This proposed framework, set to launch in October 2027, will extend FCA oversight to further cryptocurrency operations. Previous analysis noted that despite banking constraints, granting authorization to a crypto firm does not compel a retail bank to handle payment transactions for that entity.

A five-month period for applications began on September 30, 2026, extending until February 28, 2027. Holding current anti-money-laundering registration does not grant automatic authorization within this new framework. Political scrutiny regarding banking access has already emerged. During August, a cross-party parliamentary group interrogated senior executives from major banks concerning their handling of cryptocurrency entities and associated fund movements. The investigation sought specifics on denied accounts, imposed payment caps, and the criteria institutions use to evaluate crypto-related enterprises.

Policy Ambiguity Persists Despite New Regulatory Timeline

Katie Harries, head of European policy at Coinbase, criticized current restrictions that impact transfers to businesses registered with the Financial Conduct Authority (FCA). She argued that financial institutions should assess individual payments rather than applying blanket restrictions across the entire cryptocurrency industry. Additionally, the Stand With Crypto campaign urges banks to publish clearer explanations of their policies and provide customers with practical methods to challenge rejected transactions.

Stand With Crypto has formally asked the FCA and HM Treasury to explain when blanket restrictions on an entire industry are warranted under fraud prevention and financial crime rules. The report failed to quantify successful attempted crypto purchases, count failed transfers linked to suspected fraud, or determine the proportion of complaints that banks upheld.
