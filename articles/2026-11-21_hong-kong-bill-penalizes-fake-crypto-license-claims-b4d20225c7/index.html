Hong Kong Bill Penalizes Fake Crypto License Claims

Presence Editorial · November 21, 2026 · 4 min read

Under a new crypto licensing bill in Hong Kong, falsely asserting that one has applied for a license carries a maximum sentence of two years behind bars. Those making such false claims face potential imprisonment of up to two years alongside a HK$400,000 penalty, whereas using a regulated title without authorization may result in a HK$100,000 fine.

On Nov. 20, the Hong Kong government announced that it had gazetted the Virtual Asset Services Legislation (Amendment) Bill 2026, setting Dec. 2 as the date for its first reading. Financial institutions and licensed stored-value payment providers must register with the SFC if they wish to provide virtual asset services within Hong Kong.

Penalties for False Licensing Claims

The proposed legislation extends regulatory oversight by bringing crypto dealers, advisers, asset managers, and custodians under the jurisdiction of the Securities and Futures Commission (SFC), thereby expanding upon the existing licensing framework that currently covers virtual asset trading platforms.

CoinNess reported, citing Caixin, that a conviction on indictment for falsely claiming to be a license applicant without having filed one could result in imprisonment and a fine of HK$400,000, plus an additional penalty of HK$10,000 for each day the offense continues.

The draft legislation establishes a distinct criminal offense for individuals who utilize titles linked to Securities and Futures Commission-regulated virtual asset businesses without holding the necessary license or registration. Specific examples of such unauthorized titles include cryptocurrency dealer, cryptocurrency adviser, and virtual asset custodian. For this violation, the proposed penalty structure includes an initial fine of HK$100,000, which may be followed by a recurring daily surcharge of HK$2,000 for every day the offense persists.

These two clauses aim at distinct kinds of misleading statements a company could direct toward its clientele. One clause focuses on the precise designation employed to characterize the services provided, whereas the other examines whether the organization has genuinely proceeded with filing a license application. This separation aligns with the existing methodology employed by the SFC regarding its platform listings, which lists licensed exchanges by name apart from those whose applications remain under review.

While certain entities on the submission roster benefit from a transitional clause that treated them as licensed starting June 1, 2024 despite ongoing reviews, the Securities and Futures Commission warns these candidates could ultimately face license rejection and be ordered to shut down their Hong Kong operations.

Officials intend to update Hong Kong's Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorist Financing Ordinance so it encompasses four specific sectors. According to the report coverage of the legislation, firms will need either a license or SFC registration alongside meeting fit-and-proper criteria, granting administrative authority to both the Securities and Futures Commission and the Hong Kong Monetary Authority under the new framework.

Financial institutions and authorized stored-value facility providers currently report to the monetary authority. Officials state that any entity wishing to offer virtual asset services in Hong Kong must register with the SFC. These stored-value facilities encompass payment instruments enabling clients to deposit funds and maintain a balance for future transactions.

New custody regulations target firms responsible for securing client cryptocurrency holdings or managing the private keys required to transfer those assets. Previous licensing initiatives in Hong Kong outlined dealing standards based on securities dealer frameworks. A distinct set of proposals addressed custody, specifically focusing on methods for holding and safeguarding client property.

Christopher Hui, the Financial Services and the Treasury Secretary, affirmed that the regulatory framework is built upon the concept of 'same activity, same risks, same regulation.' Authorities indicated they applied global benchmarks to combat money laundering and shield investors, ensuring the new rules match those currently governing Hong Kong's financial sector.

Although regulators first thought about grouping advice and management within the proposed dealing regime, distinct categories emerged following industry feedback. Officials noted that the consultation regarding dealing and custody gathered over 190 replies before conclusions were released in December 2025, while the separate inquiry into advice and management collected 51 responses with results issued in May 2026.

Expanded Regulatory Scope and Registration

The distinction between regulatory categories hinges on who makes the investment decision, as outlined in proposals from May that covered advisers recommending specific virtual assets and managers with discretion to trade on a client's behalf. Regulators clarified that an automated service generating specific investment recommendations could fall under the advisory regime, while general market information remained distinct, including software that filters factual data for customers conducting their own research.

Regarding portfolio managers, the consultation outlined regulations that mirror Hong Kong's current framework for discretionary securities management, omitting any minimum cryptocurrency allocation threshold to classify a portfolio under the proposed rules. Consequently, a manager exercising discretion over a mix of traditional securities and virtual assets might require authorization from both regulatory frameworks, a distinction arising from separate categories established after industry feedback regarding the initial grouping of advice and management within the dealing regime.
