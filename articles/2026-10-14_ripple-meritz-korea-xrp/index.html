Ripple Adds Fourth South Korean Institutional Partner, but XRP’s Role Remains Unclear

Presence Editorial · October 14, 2026 · 8 min read

The blockchain company’s new agreement with Meritz Securities extends its reach into capital markets after partnerships spanning insurance, banking and remittances. Whether those relationships translate into activity involving XRP is a separate question.

Ripple is building a broader institutional presence in South Korea, adding one of the country's established securities firms to a series of partnerships covering different parts of its financial system. The latest agreement, with Meritz Securities, will examine the use of Ripple's digital asset custody and tokenization technology in capital markets, bringing the company's South Korean institutional partnership count to four in 2026.

The agreement adds securities brokerage to a roster that already includes an insurer, an internet-only bank and a regional bank. In April, Ripple announced partnerships with Kyobo Life Insurance and Kbank. In August, it added Jeonbuk Bank for cross-border payments. Meritz brings a different set of institutional requirements, encompassing the safekeeping of digital assets, potential tokenized securities and the operational infrastructure needed to serve investors in regulated markets.

The expansion is significant for Ripple's enterprise business. It is less conclusive for XRP, the cryptocurrency closely associated with the company. None of the details released about the Meritz partnership establish that XRP will be used as a settlement asset, that securities will be issued on the XRP Ledger, or that a new digital asset service has begun operating. The distinction is increasingly important as Ripple markets infrastructure that can serve institutions across multiple blockchains and asset types.

MERITZ ENTERS THE PICTURE

Meritz and Ripple signed their strategic partnership on October 1 at the securities firm's headquarters in Seoul's Yeouido financial district, according to Korean reporting. Meritz disclosed the arrangement on October 7, while Ripple published its own announcement on October 14. The companies plan to assess how Ripple Custody and tokenization infrastructure could fit within South Korea's existing capital-markets framework, then expand their cooperation as regulation permits.

The initial scope is exploratory. Ripple Custody is designed to help regulated financial institutions securely hold, transfer and administer digital assets. Such systems address a practical barrier to institutional adoption: custody is not simply a wallet interface but a collection of controls around private keys, authorization, transaction policies, operational oversight and recordkeeping. For firms accustomed to securities regulation, those requirements can determine whether a digital asset product is operationally feasible at all.

Meritz is reviewing several potential lines of business, including security tokens, fractional investments, digital asset trading platforms, spot cryptocurrency exchange-traded funds and Korean won-denominated stablecoins. These are areas of interest, not a list of launched products under the Ripple agreement. Nor is there an announced timetable for issuing an asset or opening a new service to customers.

Byungha Kang, managing director of Meritz Securities' strategic planning unit, said the partnership would combine the brokerage's capital-markets capabilities with Ripple's digital asset infrastructure. Ripple's Asia-Pacific managing director Fiona Murray described South Korean institutions as preparing to move from experimentation toward practical applications. Both companies emphasized a staged approach conditioned by the evolution of the country's rules.

That caution matters. An arrangement to evaluate custody and tokenization can precede a product, but it does not reveal which underlying blockchain, settlement currency, distribution model or regulatory authorization an eventual offering might use. At present, Meritz's concrete commitment is to investigate the infrastructure and work within permitted business activities.

FOUR PARTNERSHIPS, FOUR DIFFERENT USE CASES

Ripple's South Korean expansion is not a single initiative being repeated across four institutions. Each partnership addresses a different operational problem.

Kyobo Life Insurance, announced in April, is exploring tokenized government bond transactions and settlement through Ripple Custody. The attraction is a potentially more efficient process for representing, transferring and settling bond-related interests under institutional controls. The project has been described as an effort to validate transaction structures, rather than evidence that Korea's government bond market has broadly migrated onchain.

Kbank, also announced in April, is deploying institutional wallet infrastructure through Ripple Custody. Its focus is on tools for managing digital assets across networks, including multi-party computation-based security. The arrangement is about creating the capabilities that a regulated banking institution might need before offering or integrating additional digital asset services. It should not be confused with an announcement that the bank is processing XRP-denominated flows.

Jeonbuk Bank's August agreement is more directly tied to payments. The regional lender adopted Ripple Payments for international remittances, with Ripple promoting near-real-time, round-the-clock settlement for business customers such as importers, exporters and technology companies. But the branding of a Ripple Payments service alone does not specify the settlement assets used in every payment corridor. The bank's agreement did not establish a blanket requirement that transfers use XRP.

Meritz Securities extends the group into brokerage and capital markets. Unlike the other partners, its potential products could span investment issuance, tokenization, custody, trading infrastructure and fractional ownership. Yet the announced partnership remains an infrastructure evaluation, not evidence that securities have already been issued on a particular ledger.

Taken together, the four agreements create a more diversified institutional footprint than any one announcement might suggest. Insurance, banking, remittances and securities have different regulatory obligations, customer bases and transaction processes. Ripple is trying to become a technology provider across those categories, rather than relying exclusively on one cross-border payment product.

WHY A RIPPLE DEAL DOES NOT NECESSARILY MEAN XRP ADOPTION

Ripple, XRP and the XRP Ledger are related but distinct. Ripple is a company selling financial software and infrastructure. XRP is a tradable digital asset. The XRP Ledger is a blockchain that can transfer XRP and other issued assets. An institution can purchase Ripple software without committing to use XRP for its transactions, and it can use custody technology for assets on networks other than the XRP Ledger.

Ripple's own custody documentation lists support for multiple blockchain environments, including Bitcoin, Ethereum, Solana and the XRP Ledger. That flexibility is commercially useful for institutions whose requirements may include several types of assets. It also means that a new Ripple Custody customer cannot automatically be counted as a new source of XRP transaction demand.

A similar distinction applies to payments. Faster international transfers may depend on different settlement assets and local payout arrangements, and the role of XRP must be established from the actual product design rather than inferred from the provider's corporate identity. For tokenization, the blockchain used to record an asset, the currency used to settle it and the custody platform used to administer it can all be separate choices.

These differences have implications for how investors interpret partnership announcements. An additional securities company using Ripple technology may create software or service revenue for Ripple. It does not, by itself, demonstrate incremental purchases of XRP, sustained onchain activity involving the token or a corresponding source of price support.

The Meritz announcement provides no publicly disclosed XRP settlement flow, no identified security issuance on the XRP Ledger and no production-launch date tied to XRP. That absence is not proof that XRP will never be involved. It simply leaves a central part of the commercial architecture undecided or undisclosed.

KOREA'S INSTITUTIONAL MARKET IS CHANGING IN STAGES

South Korea has long been an important cryptocurrency market, with active retail trading alongside a more restrictive institutional and regulatory environment. The growth of tokenized securities, investment products and stablecoin services depends on more than technical capability. It also requires rules governing issuers, intermediaries, custody, investor protections and the treatment of tokenized claims.

That helps explain why the Meritz agreement emphasizes existing securities activities and gradual expansion. A brokerage may want the ability to hold or administer tokenized assets before it is permitted to distribute all the products under consideration. It may also need to connect new systems to established compliance, accounting and customer-control processes.

The same logic applies to Ripple's wider relationships. Kyobo's bond settlement experiment addresses one institutional workflow. Kbank's wallets provide another piece of infrastructure. Jeonbuk's payments partnership targets cross-border business transfers. Meritz examines the capital-markets layer. None should be mistaken for a comprehensive rollout of blockchain-based finance across the Korean economy.

For Ripple, however, winning a place in these early design discussions has strategic value. Infrastructure choices made during pilots and system planning can influence later procurement decisions, integrations and institutional workflows. A provider that already serves several categories of financial institution may have more opportunities to expand its products as those institutions move from evaluation to production.

This is an opportunity, not a guaranteed result. The agreements differ in maturity, regulatory exposure and commercial scope. Public announcements do not provide comparable measures of client revenue, transaction volumes or implementation costs for all four relationships, making it premature to translate the partnership count into a precise estimate of Ripple's business in South Korea.

WHAT WILL SHOW WHETHER XRP BENEFITS

The next evidence to watch is operational rather than promotional. For Meritz, the important milestones would be a named product, the disclosure of its issuing or settlement blockchain, applicable regulatory approval and an announced customer launch. If the firm issues securities on the XRP Ledger, or uses XRP as part of settlement, those details would establish a connection that the current agreement does not.

For Jeonbuk Bank, greater transparency on live payment corridors, settlement assets and production volumes would clarify the relationship between Ripple's payment business and XRP. For Kyobo, the outcome of bond settlement work and the specific technology used would matter more than the existence of the partnership alone. For Kbank, the networks and assets supported in active institutional workflows would offer a more useful indication of adoption.

Ripple has now established announced relationships across several major segments of South Korea's financial system in a matter of months. That is a measurable expansion of its institutional distribution and a potential foundation for future custody, payments and tokenization business.

What remains unmeasured is how much of that business, if it reaches production, will flow through the XRP Ledger or require the XRP token itself. The distinction between selling blockchain infrastructure and creating demand for a particular cryptocurrency is not a technical footnote. It is the question that will determine whether Ripple's growing Korean footprint produces a corresponding onchain role for XRP.
