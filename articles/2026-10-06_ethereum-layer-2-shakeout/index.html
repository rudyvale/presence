Ethereum’s Layer 2 Shakeout Begins as Pudgy Penguins’ Abstract and Blast Shut Down

Presence Editorial · October 6, 2026 · 11 min read

Two prominent Ethereum scaling networks are winding down within days of each other, exposing the growing divide between blockchain activity, ecosystem adoption and the economics of operating an independent chain.

For much of Ethereum’s recent history, launching a dedicated blockchain was treated as a natural progression for successful crypto businesses. Exchanges, gaming studios, NFT collections and consumer platforms increasingly pursued their own Layer 2 networks, encouraged by infrastructure that made blockchain deployment faster and less expensive than building an independent Layer 1.

That expansion is beginning to face a more fundamental economic test.

Abstract, the consumer-focused Ethereum Layer 2 developed by Igloo Inc., the parent company of Pudgy Penguins, announced on October 6 that it would shut down its network on December 15, 2026. The decision follows nearly two years of efforts to establish a blockchain around one of the cryptocurrency industry's most recognizable consumer brands, despite hundreds of millions of processed transactions and billions of dollars in cumulative trading activity.

Just four days earlier, Paradigm-backed Blast announced plans to wind down its own Ethereum scaling network, citing operating expenses that exceeded revenue and the absence of a credible path toward sustainability.

The two closures, involving projects with different audiences, commercial strategies and financial backers, are raising a broader question about Ethereum's scaling ecosystem: whether the industry's capacity to create new blockchains has outpaced its ability to support them economically.

Abstract’s Consumer Crypto Experiment Comes to an End

Abstract entered the market with several advantages that most emerging blockchain networks lack. Its parent company, Igloo, controlled Pudgy Penguins, an NFT franchise that had expanded beyond crypto-native audiences into physical merchandise, entertainment and mainstream retail.

Under CEO Luca Netz, Pudgy Penguins had developed a recognizable intellectual property business, with collectible toys appearing in major retailers including Walmart and Target. The brand offered an established community, consumer distribution channels and a potentially valuable starting point for introducing ordinary users to blockchain applications.

In June 2024, Igloo acquired Frame, an Ethereum Layer 2 project originally focused on NFTs. The following month, it secured more than $11 million in funding led by Founders Fund to support development of what would become Abstract.

The network launched its mainnet in January 2025, positioning itself as a blockchain designed primarily for consumer applications rather than the financial speculation and trading activity that had defined much of the industry's early growth.

Abstract's strategy centered on making crypto applications more accessible. Gaming experiences, digital collectibles, consumer loyalty products and entertainment platforms were expected to bring users onchain through familiar experiences rather than complex financial interfaces.

The approach produced substantial activity.

According to figures reported around the shutdown announcement, Abstract processed more than 325 million transactions, accumulated over four million wallets and facilitated approximately $6 billion in decentralized exchange trading volume. More than 144 applications were deployed on the network, while businesses operating in its ecosystem reportedly generated over $40 million in revenue.

The project also reported onboarding more than 400,000 users through consumer-oriented partnerships involving brands such as Disney and Red Bull Racing.

These figures distinguish Abstract from the many blockchain initiatives that struggled to generate meaningful adoption after launching. Its ecosystem attracted users, developers, brands and commercial activity, yet those achievements ultimately failed to produce a sustainable business for the underlying network.

In his October 6 statement, Netz disclosed that Igloo had supported Abstract financially for approximately 18 months, accumulating losses in the tens of millions of dollars. Continuing to subsidize the network would have meant diverting additional resources from the company's more established Pudgy Penguins operations.

Igloo also considered whether a token issuance or initial coin offering could provide additional financing. Netz said the company rejected that option because it lacked confidence that an Abstract token would have sufficient underlying demand.

Rather than introduce another speculative asset to extend the network's operating life, the company elected to discontinue the blockchain and concentrate its resources on Pudgy Penguins, the franchise's NFT collections and its existing PENGU token.

For Abstract users, the decision has immediate practical consequences. Assets must be bridged away from the network before December 15, after which remaining funds may become inaccessible. The project has directed users toward its migration infrastructure to facilitate withdrawals.

The shutdown does not mean Pudgy Penguins itself is closing. Instead, it represents Igloo's withdrawal from a capital-intensive infrastructure strategy that had become increasingly difficult to justify alongside its established consumer business.

Why Millions of Transactions Were Not Enough

Abstract's closure illustrates a central economic problem confronting application-specific blockchain networks: user activity and commercial success do not automatically translate into profitability at the infrastructure level.

A game can generate revenue through digital purchases. A marketplace can charge trading commissions. A consumer application can build a substantial business around subscriptions, merchandise or digital services. Yet the blockchain supporting those products captures only the portion of economic activity represented by network fees and other protocol-level revenue mechanisms.

In an ecosystem designed around inexpensive transactions, that distinction becomes particularly important.

Ethereum Layer 2 networks were developed to reduce the costs associated with executing transactions directly on Ethereum. By processing transactions separately and publishing compressed data or proofs to the underlying blockchain, they can offer significantly lower fees while retaining varying degrees of Ethereum-based security.

Those lower costs benefit applications and their users, but they can also constrain revenue available to network operators.

An independent Layer 2 must still support infrastructure, engineering, security, developer services, ecosystem development and operational maintenance. Depending on its architecture, additional costs may include data availability, settlement, proving systems and sequencer operations.

As a result, large transaction counts can coexist with relatively modest network revenue, particularly when activity consists of inexpensive interactions rather than higher-value financial transactions.

Data from DeFiLlama around the shutdown announcement illustrated the disparity. In one 24-hour reporting period cited by CoinDesk, applications operating on Abstract generated approximately $39,000 in revenue, while the blockchain itself collected roughly $3,900 in chain fees.

The figures represent a single reporting window rather than a comprehensive assessment of Abstract's operating finances. Nevertheless, they demonstrate how an ecosystem can support commercial applications without generating proportional revenue for the infrastructure underneath them.

Abstract's strategic specialization may also have contributed to the difficulty.

At launch, the network deliberately emphasized entertainment and consumer applications over decentralized finance. Netz publicly encouraged developers pursuing conventional DeFi products to consider other networks, including Arbitrum and Berachain, while Abstract concentrated on simpler consumer experiences.

The decision gave Abstract a differentiated identity in an increasingly crowded Layer 2 market. However, it also limited the network's exposure to financial activities that frequently attract persistent liquidity, sophisticated market participants and higher-value transactions.

Without a sufficiently developed lending, trading and liquidity infrastructure, consumer applications can generate considerable engagement while remaining economically disconnected from the broader onchain financial system.

In explaining the closure, Abstract cited stagnant growth, insufficient liquidity, a restricted DeFi ecosystem and limited institutional participation.

The result was a network with recognizable distribution and substantial cumulative usage, but without the recurring economic activity necessary to justify continued financial support from its parent company.

Blast’s Collapse Reveals a Different Version of the Same Problem

Abstract's decision came less than a week after Blast, another prominent Ethereum Layer 2, announced that its economics had become unsustainable.

Blast emerged during the previous crypto market cycle with a distinctly different proposition. Developed by the team associated with NFT marketplace Blur and backed by investors including Paradigm and Standard Crypto, the network attracted attention by offering native yield on ETH and stablecoin balances.

Its model appealed directly to crypto-native capital. Instead of requiring users to actively deploy their assets into external yield-generating protocols, Blast sought to make returns an integrated feature of the network itself.

The strategy initially attracted extraordinary liquidity. Before its mainnet became publicly accessible in February 2024, Blast had already accumulated more than $2 billion in deposited assets, supported by early-access incentives and expectations surrounding its eventual token distribution.

At its peak, Blast ranked among Ethereum's largest Layer 2 ecosystems by capital deposited.

However, the network struggled to preserve that position as incentives diminished and liquidity moved elsewhere. By the time its shutdown was announced on October 2, 2026, its decentralized finance total value locked had fallen to approximately $32 million, according to figures reported by The Block.

Blast's team acknowledged that the network's operating expenses had overtaken its revenue, leaving no realistic commercial justification for maintaining the infrastructure.

The closure also introduced a withdrawal process for remaining users. Blast requested that assets be returned to Ethereum mainnet, with October 26 established as the deadline for withdrawals through its standard interface. Following that date, users would need to interact directly with bridge contracts on Ethereum to recover their holdings.

Although Blast and Abstract pursued different audiences, their trajectories reveal a similar structural weakness.

Blast attracted liquidity primarily through financial incentives and native yield. Abstract attracted consumers through recognizable intellectual property, entertainment applications and brand partnerships.

Both generated substantial attention during their early development. Neither succeeded in establishing a durable economic model capable of supporting an independent blockchain after the initial growth phase.

Their shutdowns suggest that attracting users or capital may be a fundamentally different challenge from retaining enough valuable activity to sustain a network over multiple market cycles.

Ethereum’s Expanding Layer 2 Economy Faces Consolidation

The difficulties confronting Abstract and Blast are unfolding against the backdrop of a much larger expansion in Ethereum's scaling infrastructure.

The development of rollup technologies, modular blockchain infrastructure and increasingly accessible chain deployment platforms transformed the economics of creating new networks. Projects that once would have needed to build applications on existing infrastructure could instead pursue dedicated execution environments, customized governance arrangements and direct control over portions of their transaction revenue.

For exchanges and financial platforms, the appeal included greater control over user experience and the possibility of internalizing economic activity that would otherwise accrue to external networks.

Gaming companies and consumer brands saw opportunities to create infrastructure around their own communities, potentially integrating payments, digital assets and application ecosystems within a controlled environment.

Infrastructure providers, meanwhile, made it progressively easier for projects to launch chains using established software frameworks rather than developing entirely new blockchain architectures.

The result was an increasingly fragmented competitive landscape, in which numerous networks pursued overlapping groups of developers, users, applications and capital.

Yet the availability of blockchain infrastructure did not eliminate the network effects that traditionally influence financial markets.

Liquidity remains particularly difficult to distribute across large numbers of independent environments. Lending protocols require sufficient deposits and borrowing demand. Decentralized exchanges depend on market depth, trading activity and market makers. Developers benefit from access to established financial primitives, distribution channels and users with assets already deployed.

Those advantages often reinforce one another.

A network with substantial liquidity can attract applications, while those applications encourage further deposits and user participation. Emerging networks must overcome this cycle, frequently through developer grants, token incentives, subsidized transactions or other forms of financial support.

Such incentives can create early momentum, but they do not guarantee that activity will remain once subsidies are reduced.

The consequences extend beyond individual blockchain operators. Every additional execution environment can introduce new liquidity pools, bridging requirements and infrastructure dependencies, potentially increasing fragmentation even as Ethereum's aggregate transaction capacity expands.

However, consolidation would not necessarily require every smaller Layer 2 to shut down. Networks could also migrate applications to larger ecosystems, share infrastructure, adopt interoperability systems or reconsider whether operating an independent chain provides enough strategic value.

The emerging distinction is between blockchains that create meaningful economic advantages for their operators and those whose primary contribution could potentially be delivered more efficiently as applications on an existing network.

A More Selective Future for Ethereum Scaling

The closures of Abstract and Blast should not be interpreted as evidence that Ethereum's broader scaling strategy has failed.

Layer 2 networks continue to serve an important technical purpose by expanding transaction capacity and reducing the costs associated with using Ethereum-based applications. The ability to process more activity outside Ethereum's main execution environment remains central to the network's long-term development.

What is becoming less certain is the commercial assumption that every successful crypto company, consumer brand or application ecosystem should eventually operate its own blockchain.

Abstract represents a particularly revealing case because it possessed advantages that are difficult to replicate. Pudgy Penguins had already demonstrated an ability to reach audiences beyond speculative crypto trading, establish relationships with mainstream retailers and convert digital intellectual property into a consumer-facing business.

Despite those strengths, its parent company concluded that maintaining a separate blockchain consumed more resources than the infrastructure could economically justify.

Blast approached the market from the opposite direction, using yield and financial incentives to attract substantial capital before declining activity undermined its operating model.

Neither outcome establishes that consumer-focused chains or yield-oriented networks are inherently unviable. Both projects also faced execution challenges and competitive conditions specific to their businesses.

Together, however, they provide a clearer illustration of the limitations of the previous expansion cycle.

As Ethereum's Layer 2 ecosystem matures, the competitive advantage may shift away from the ability to launch a network and toward the ability to retain liquidity, generate recurring revenue and support applications that users continue to value without substantial incentives.

For developers, that could make established networks with deeper financial ecosystems more attractive than launching independent infrastructure. For investors, it may place greater emphasis on sustainable protocol economics rather than cumulative transaction counts or headline wallet metrics.

For consumer brands, Abstract's experience presents a more specific consideration: owning the relationship with users does not necessarily require owning the blockchain on which those users transact.

Ethereum's next phase of scaling may therefore be defined as much by the consolidation of its existing networks as by the introduction of new ones. The technology required to launch a Layer 2 has become increasingly accessible. Building an independent network with lasting economic value remains considerably more difficult.
