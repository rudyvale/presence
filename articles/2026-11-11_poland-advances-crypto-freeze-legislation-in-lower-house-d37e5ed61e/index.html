Poland Advances Crypto Freeze Legislation in Lower House

Presence Editorial · November 11, 2026 · 4 min read

On November 6, the Polish lower chamber approved a sanctions measure targeting cryptocurrency freezes with a tally of 240 in favor against 202 opposition, alongside one abstention and 17 absent votes, before forwarding the draft to the Senate.

This proposed law designates digital currency holdings as financial assets eligible for freezing regarding specific individuals and entities, ensuring that judicial appeals do not immediately halt limitations derived from the interior minister's sanctions roster; meanwhile, the initiative focuses on enforcing sanctions rules even as distinct crypto licensing statutes face ongoing political contention.

Legislative Progress and Scope

The Sejm approved the government-backed Act on Restrictive Measures during its Nov. 6 sitting, establishing a national framework for authorities to impose sanctions and restrict money, property, and other assets. As the report previously reported on Nov. 9, this legislation still requires Senate consideration and presidential approval before becoming law.

The initial version of the proposal placed digital currencies inside the category of financial funds, grouping them with bank deposits, account balances, securities, dividends, and credit. This definition aligns with Article 3 of the European Union's Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation, or MiCA, which characterizes freezing as stopping movements, transfers, use, or transactions that alter an asset's ownership, location, value, or availability.

The draft law aims to penalize persons and organizations placed on a sanctions roster overseen by the interior minister. Eligibility for this list rests on backing sanctioned nations, possessing particular financial or personal ties, or engaging in external interference that threatens Poland's security.

Financial institutions and trading platforms keeping funds for those on the list must adhere to relevant limits. According to reporting from Nov. 9, handling self-custodied wallets requires pinpointing specific assets before limiting interactions with sanctioned individuals.

Under Articles 8 and 9 of the proposed legislation, an administrative mechanism is created wherein the interior minister formulates listing determinations and disseminates them via the ministry's official public information bulletin. While such a directive does not grant officials access to private keys required for transferring assets from individual wallets, it may bar service providers from executing transactions or rendering assets accessible to sanctioned individuals.

Once published, these rulings take effect immediately, granting affected entities the right to seek review in an administrative court; however, the draft maintains that any disputed ruling stays active until a final judgment is rendered. The framework empowers the minister to define the specific funds or economic resources subject to limits and allows for restricted disclosure when decisions rest on national security or public order considerations.

Article 10 of the original draft establishes exceptions where authorities may authorize access to frozen resources for essential purposes such as food, housing, medicine, taxes, utilities, reasonable legal fees, and specific maintenance costs. This access is not automatic but requires a separate decision accompanied by defined conditions.

During a parliamentary debate on Sept. 3, Deputy Interior Minister Czesław Mroczek noted that Poland's current national system functions under an April 2022 law designed to address support for Russia's aggression against Ukraine. He further explained that the government's project description aims to consolidate scattered sanctions provisions into a unified framework that organizes responsibilities, information sharing, supervision, and penalties.

Deputy Interior Minister Czesław Mroczek stated that a specific list contained 554 entries, comprising 119 businesses and 435 individuals. Among the businesses on this list, 99 were linked to Russian capital while 20 were connected to Belarusian capital. The government asserts that the new legislation will enable national measures to address situations extending beyond Russia and Belarus, noting that the proposal implements EU Directive 2024/1226 regarding offenses and penalties for violations of EU sanctions.

Enforcement Mechanisms and Exceptions

After President Karol Nawrocki exercised his third veto on June 11, Poland's push for domestic crypto oversight took a unique legislative path. The head of state challenged the government's suggested framework yet voiced backing for robust consumer safeguards. When the Sejm tried to overturn this veto in September, the motion fell short; the chamber recorded 241 supportive votes, 198 opposing ones, and three abstentions. Since 266 votes were necessary to pass the measure again, the bill was defeated. That rejected proposal would have named the Polish Financial Supervision Authority as the nation's crypto regulator.

Within the United States, the Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control enforces sanctions rules that apply equally to digital assets and conventional currency. This guidance mandates that individuals under US jurisdiction must freeze assets owned by sanctioned entities and refrain from engaging in restricted activities, such as those designed to circumvent these measures.
