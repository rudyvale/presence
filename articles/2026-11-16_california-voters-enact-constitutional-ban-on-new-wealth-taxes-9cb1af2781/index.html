California Voters Enact Constitutional Ban on New Wealth Taxes

Presence Editorial · November 16, 2026 · 4 min read

California voters have enacted Proposition 42, a constitutional amendment that prohibits the state from imposing new taxes on the ownership of personal assets such as cryptocurrencies, stocks, retirement accounts, and business interests. The measure passed with just over 51% support in the election held recently.

While this ban applies to asset ownership, existing taxes on realized cryptocurrency gains remain unaffected by the amendment. In contrast, voters rejected Proposition 40, which would have levied a one-time wealth tax of up to 5% on billionaires holding more than $1 billion in covered assets. The campaign supporting Proposition 42 received backing from technology executives including Google cofounder Sergey Brin.

Constitutional Shield for Personal Assets Approved

Based on election results monitored by the Associated Press, the measure received slightly more than 51% backing versus almost 49% opposition, with approximately 96% of ballots tallied. The news agency declared the race outcome on Nov. 13, a span of ten days following the midterm elections in California.

This ruling bars state legislators from levying fresh levies that rely exclusively on asset worth, encompassing digital currencies, stock holdings, or other financial instruments. The limitations extend to new personal property taxes and largely forbid retroactive impositions, allowing only specific exceptions tied to declared emergencies.

Proposition 42 establishes that the state is barred from levying new taxes strictly on the ownership of financial holdings, which encompasses digital currencies. This safeguard applies to investment accounts, intellectual property rights, equity in businesses, retirement funds, and personal items, noting that real estate does not fall within the amendment's scope regarding personal property.

Regarding those who hold cryptocurrency, the key difference is separating asset ownership from taxable earnings derived from them. The state continues to tax income generated by financial investments, such as profits from selling holdings. Current federal guidelines for crypto taxation mean that disposing of coins, exchanging tokens, and accepting specific digital rewards may create tax liabilities.

Proposition 42 stops the state from levying a distinct tax on crypto holdings or their value, covering assets that remain unsold by investors. It does not wipe out current taxes nor does it establish a fresh exemption for trading profits. Current levies stay intact while new retroactive charges are capped, barring cases of declared emergencies.

In other American states, Illinois plans to start a 0.2% levy on digital assets by Jan. 1, 2027. This charge targets services related to exchanging, moving, or storing specific cryptocurrencies, with the amount derived from asset values. Recent voting concluded after a tight race regarding Proposition 40, which sought a single wealth tax reaching 5% for those and trusts owning over $1 billion in qualifying assets.

The proposed levy under Proposition 40 would have encompassed businesses, securities, intellectual property, artwork, and collectibles while explicitly excluding real estate and specific retirement accounts. Revenue generated from this measure was intended to be distributed with 90% directed toward health care initiatives and the remaining 10% allocated to food assistance or education programs.

Jesse Powell, co-founder of Kraken, and David Sacks, the White House official overseeing AI and crypto policy, voiced resistance to the initiative. They maintained that imposing taxes on assets held in digital currencies or private enterprises might compel owners to liquidate holdings. Additionally, opponents suggested that such fiscal measures could drive affluent individuals to relocate away from the state.

In the final tally, Proposition 40 was defeated by a margin of roughly 52% against it. A rival initiative, Proposition 42, entered the race with the specific aim of nullifying the billionaire levy should both measures succeed and the latter garner sufficient yes votes. While the standalone wealth tax did not pass, the passage of Proposition 42 created a constitutional barrier against comparable state levies in the future.

The initiative to put Proposition 42 on the ballot was driven by Building a Better California, an organization backed by Google co-founder Sergey Brin along with various venture capitalists and tech leaders. Additional backing came from groups advocating for small enterprises, older adults, firefighters, and police officers. Brian Marvel, who leads the Peace Officers Research Association of California, expressed approval of the outcome, noting that citizens had protected retirement funds from becoming a target for future taxation.

Existing Income Taxes Remain Intact Despite Ownership Ban

Proposition 42 grants Californians security regarding their retirement savings by prohibiting new wealth taxes on crypto and other assets, a outcome Marvel described as ensuring members know their funds will remain available when needed. Susan Shelley of the Howard Jarvis Association noted that the vote reflected voter concern about potential future taxation of retirement accounts.

Advocates for Proposition 40 have contemplated bringing forward another billionaire wealth tax plan during the 2028 election cycle, yet implementing such a measure would necessitate that voters modify or reverse the limitations set forth in Proposition 42 prior to California levying a new levy on personal asset holdings.
